Rethinking LLM Jailbreak Evaluation: Continuous Risk Landscapes Beyond Binary Outcomes
Authors: Yukai Zhou, Wenjie Wang
First: 2026-10-28T10:23:27+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-28T10:23:27+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
A binary success indicator is a natural representation for discriminative tasks such as image classification under adversarial perturbations, where each input is mapped to a single prediction. However, generative tasks are fundamentally different: an LLM jailbreak prompt induces a distribution over open-ended trajectories, while a sampled response reveals only one path. Yet LLM jailbreak evaluation typically retains the binary response indicator underlying attack success rate, obscuring the surrounding structure of possible generations. We introduce a continuous, context-conditioned risk landscape over first-token branches, estimated by fixing candidate openings and repeatedly sampling their continuations. This continuous risk-landscape perspective reveals two complementary properties: current decoder exposure and safe rerouting capacity, which have been obscured by binary-indicator-based ASR. The resulting representation also provides a common basis for comparing clean prompts, attacked variants, and attack strength against a known adaptive defense. Across multiple target models and jailbreak attacks, we uncover risky branches beneath apparently safe outputs, identify qualitatively different ways attacks reshape the model's opening distribution, and show that attacks strongest before a defense do not necessarily remain the strongest after the adaptive defense reacts. These findings recast jailbreaks as deformations of the surrounding continuation-risk landscape, establishing a more informative foundation for understanding jailbreak behavior and developing safer language models.

Spend Where It Counts: Dynamic Budget Allocation for Multi-Turn LLM Evaluation
Authors: Shai Feldman, Yaniv Romano
First: 2026-05-07T17:25:15+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T09:51:04+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Evaluating and predicting the performance of large language models (LLMs) in multi-turn conversational settings is critical yet computationally expensive; key events---e.g., jailbreaks or successful task completion by an agent---often emerge only after repeated interactions. These events might be rare and, under any feasible computational budget, remain unobserved. Recent conformal survival frameworks construct reliable lower predictive bounds (LPBs) on the number of iterations to trigger the event of interest, but rely on static budget allocation that prohibits adaptivity in multi-turn setups. To address this, we introduce Dynamic Allocation via PRojected Optimization (DAPRO), a theoretically valid dynamic budget allocation framework for bounding the time-to-event in multi-turn LLM interactions. We prove that DAPRO satisfies the expected budget constraint and provides distribution-free, finite-sample coverage guarantees without requiring the conditional independence between censoring and event times assumed by prior conformal survival approaches. A key theoretical contribution is a novel coverage bound that scales with the square root of the mean censoring weight over only a subset of the samples rather than the worst-case weight, yielding tighter guarantees than prior work. Furthermore, DAPRO can be employed to obtain unbiased estimates of population-level evaluation metrics, such as the jailbreak rate, under limited computing resources. Comprehensive experiments across agentic task success, LLM jailbreaks, toxic content generation, and RAG hallucinations demonstrate that DAPRO achieves coverage closer to the nominal level with lower variance than static baselines, while not exceeding the budget constraint in expectation. The Price of Safety: Benign-Case Utility and Token Overhead of Memory-Poisoning Defenses in LLM Agents
Authors: Pritom Bhowmik
First: 2026-09-19T06:39:21+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-22T08:33:17+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Memory-poisoning defenses for LLM agents are typically evaluated by how well they stop attacks, even though almost all traffic they encounter is benign. We measure their cost when no attack is present. Our harness holds the memory backend, retrieval, answer prompt, and judge constant, changing only the defense. We test four defenses on half of LoCoMo: five long conversations with 750 turns and 255 questions per run, repeated three times each. Replication proved necessary: one run showed a reranker cost on adversarial questions that the other two did not. The three write-time defenses (sanitization, provenance checking, and LLM-based anomaly detection) never fired on benign traffic. We detect no accuracy cost from them at a resolution of about $\pm$4 points; anomaly detection adds 1.2% token overhead. The read-time reranker, with 2.7% token overhead, fired on 9.9% of its memory decisions and withheld memory from 45% of questions. It lowered core accuracy by 4.4 points (95% CI [-9.0, -0.05]; McNemar p = 0.064). The effect is borderline in magnitude, but its direction held across all six runs that used the reranker. A blind audit found content-based justification for only 2 of 50 sampled drops. Stacking all four defenses raised token overhead to 4.1% but did not compound the accuracy cost. In our runs, cost tracked what each defense judged and how much context it used, rather than whether it used an LLM. Attack success rate reveals none of this, so benign cost should be reported alongside it. We release the harness, labels, and per-run outputs.

Quality-Diversity Red-Teaming: Automated Generation of High-Quality and Diverse Attackers for Large Language Models
Authors: Ren-Jian Wang, Ke Xue, Zeyu Qin, Ziniu Li, Sheng Tang, Hao-Tian Li, Shengcai Liu, Zhi Yu, Yuanpeng Tan, Chao Qian
First: 2025-06-08T13:07:41+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T07:48:18+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Ensuring the safety and robustness of large language models (LLMs) is a fundamental challenge and a critical prerequisite for the responsible deployment of artificial intelligence. Red-teaming, a systematic framework to identify adversarial prompts that elicit harmful responses from target LLMs, has emerged as a crucial safety evaluation paradigm. Within this framework, the diversity of adversarial prompts is critical for a comprehensive safety assessment. However, previous red-teaming approaches often pursue diversity through simplistic metrics such as word frequency or sentence embedding similarity, which may not capture meaningful variation in attack strategies. In addition, the common practice of training a single attacker model restricts coverage across all potential attack styles and risk categories. This paper introduces Quality-Diversity Red-Teaming (QDRT), a new framework designed to address these limitations. QDRT achieves goal-driven diversity through behavior-conditioned training and implements a behavioral replay buffer in an open-ended manner. In addition, it trains multiple specialized attackers capable of generating high-quality attacks across diverse styles and risk categories. Our empirical evaluation demonstrates that QDRT generates attacks that are both more diverse and more effective against a wide range of target LLMs, including open-source models \texttt{GPT-2}, \texttt{Llama-3}, \texttt{Gemma-2}, \texttt{Qwen2.5}, and commercial models \texttt{GPT-4.1} and \texttt{GPT-5-Chat}. This work advances the field of LLM safety by providing a systematic and effective approach to automated red-teaming, ultimately supporting the responsible deployment of LLMs. Our code is available at https://github.com/lamda-bbo/QDRT.

Beyond Binary Labels: Diagnostic Evaluation of LLM Safety with Prompt-Level Rubrics
Authors: Hongyu Li, Xinlong Huang, Zhan Yu, Jing Zhao, Zhe Zhao, Juntao Zhong, Xiong Kuang, Rui Yuan, ZhenXiang Yan, Huilin Xu, Haitao Yu, Lilin Wang, Rui Chen, Blaze Chen, Fengzong Lian, Renfen Hu

Comments: 42 pages, 9 figures, 24 tables. Dataset will be publicly released at https://github.com/Tencent-Hunyuan/DiagSafe
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Current LLM safety benchmarks predominantly rely on binary safe or unsafe labels, lacking explanatory depth and suffering from low consistency across automated judges. To address this, we introduce DiagSafe, a diagnostic evaluation framework that moves beyond binary classification by employing prompt-level rubrics. Grounded in globally applicable AI safety policies, each rubric provides explicit criteria along two dimensions: safety, which targets concrete harms with specific exemptions to suppress false positives, and usefulness, which independently evaluates instruction fulfillment. To ensure a robust and realistic evaluation, we construct a bilingual (English-Chinese) benchmark grounded in a comprehensive 84-label safety taxonomy, consisting of two subsets: DiagBase, which covers single- and multi-turn prompts with direct harmful queries and template-based jailbreaks, and DiagAttack, which embeds harmful intents into complex instruction-following tasks to form challenging instruction attacks. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DiagSafe significantly enhances the reliability of LLM-as-a-judge evaluation, achieving 95.34% agreement with human annotations -- substantially higher than the 69%-87% achieved by the baselines -- and high inter-judge consistency (Cohen's $κ$ = 0.864). Furthermore, applying our framework reveals that leading LLMs differ markedly in their unsafe rates, and each model also exhibits unique vulnerabilities along different dimensions. In addition, we observe that models are particularly susceptible in Chinese and multi-turn contexts. Ultimately, DiagSafe provides a reliable and explainable tool for auditing and optimizing LLM alignment.

Fooling LLM Safety Guards into Judging the Wrong Text
Authors: Utkarsh Kanwat
First: 2026-10-20T22:00:29+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T22:00:29+00:00
Comments: 21 pages (13 main body), 2 figures, 8 tables. Code: https://github.com/ukanwat/guardrebind ; model: https://huggingface.co/ukanwat/shieldstral-1.0-3b-rebinding-fix
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Safety guards check content before an LLM application acts on it, and most paste that content into a plain-text prompt. Content that imitates the prompt can append a second task, and the guard answers that one instead. We call this target rebinding. Our explanation is that the guard grades the last complete task in its input, here a harmless decoy, so the harmful request goes through unchanged. In a pre-registered test, an appended forged query and decoy clear all 75 held-out AdvBench requests that Shieldstral-1.0-3B flags. The guard is not persuaded: a directive to ignore the text above clears none of them, and in a follow-up run a harmful decoy stops the attack. On our GuardRebind benchmark, each of five guards has an attack family, chosen per guard, that clears at least 93% of its flagged items. Rebinding explains this best on Shieldstral and gpt-oss-safeguard; on ShieldGemma and Llama Guard 3, generic evasion and trailing text respectively do part of the work, and Qwen3Guard falls mainly to a forgery of its whole task. Training the guard to score content no lower than its most harmful part cuts attack success from 75% to 0.8-1.6% on attack families, decoys and forged queries held out of training. All of these attacks append the forged structure after the request, and we added the held-out-decoy evaluation after a first round of test runs. Clean AUROC falls by at most 0.01, but our test of non-inferiority is inconclusive. A score-guided search still beats this trained guard on 47 of 50 items, usually by placing forged structure before the request. On about half of the items where the search wins, the same search also finds benign text, with no forged structure, that clears the guard.

Polarity Blindness in LLM Safety: A Systematic Study of Negation, Antonym Substitution, and Adversarial Framing
Authors: Diego Miguel Lozano, Erik Derner, Juan Antonio Pérez-Ortiz, Nuria Oliver
First: 2026-10-20T09:22:50+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T09:22:50+00:00
Comments: Code and data: https://github.com/dmlls/llm-safety-polarity-blindness
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Negation remains a persistent challenge for language models across NLP tasks and modalities. We show that this weakness can undermine LLM safety guardrails through a structural vulnerability we term polarity blindness: the failure to recognize harmful intent after polarity-altering linguistic transformations. We study two forms of polarity inversion: grammatical negation and antonym replacement, and use synonym replacement as a polarity-preserving control. We further test whether an adversarial textual wrapper amplifies these failures. Using a 260-prompt benchmark across 10 state-of-the-art LLMs and 3 languages (English, Spanish, Czech), stratified by harmfulness potential and evaluated on a validated 0-5 harm scale, we find that polarity blindness affects most evaluated models, with mean harm scores reaching 4.12 for the most vulnerable model. The effect persists across typologically differing negation systems, suggesting that polarity blindness reflects a broader limitation in semantic robustness rather than a language- or grammar-specific failure.

When LLMs Manipulate: Measuring Behavioral, Social, and Temporal Effects
Authors: Sadia Asif, Israel Antonio Rosales Laguan, Shumaila Asif, Haris Khan, Muneeb Asif, Muhammad Zeeshan Karamat
First: 2026-10-20T03:41:05+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T03:41:05+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can influence user trust and decision-making through subtle linguistic cues, yet the behavioral effects of manipulative outputs remain poorly quantified. We introduce a behavioral computing framework for measuring \textit{behavioral vulnerability}, defined as users' susceptibility to manipulative AI responses. Building on DarkPatterns-LLM benchmark, we generate controlled manipulative stimuli using Manipulation Gradient Descent (MGD), targeting biases associated with authority, urgency, and social proof. We analyze participant responses using the Multi-Stakeholder Impact Assessment Network (MSIAN) to model cross-user influence and the Temporal Harm Propagation Model (THPM) to characterize the persistence of behavioral effects over time. In a study involving 120 participants, manipulative framing produced significant changes in trust calibration and compliance ($p < 0.01$), with effects also propagating across social contexts. These findings highlight the need to extend LLM safety evaluation beyond model outputs to measurable behavioral consequences and provide a computational framework for quantifying individual, social, and temporal risks arising from manipulative AI interactions.

LLM-Attack Suite: A Unified Red-Team Framework for Privacy Evaluation of Split and Distributed LLM Systems
Authors: Georgios Politis, Evangelos Pappas
First: 2026-10-15T22:53:45+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-19T23:03:49+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Split language models keep input text on a client but send intermediate representations to a server. Attacks can reconstruct text from these messages. Comparing privacy evaluations is difficult when attackers see different messages, know different model weights or use different datasets and recovery measures. We present LLM-Attack Suite, a framework that records these choices and lets researchers check, compare and extend an evaluation. Compatible attacks reuse recorded messages, and their predictions are saved for independent scoring. The scoring rule requires a linked undefended reference before a defence can pass. Reports also identify attacks that remain untested. In a controlled GPT-2 experiment, adding gradients returned during training increased token recovery from 94.20% to 97.38% and exact-document recovery from 13.71% to 37.77%. The 95% interval for the token gain lay above the two-percentage-point threshold fixed before testing. This experiment is shared with a companion study; here it tests how the evaluation responds to a change in attacker access. Further studies measure how corpus choice and compression defences affect recovery, compare an adapted published attack with its reference implementation, and test the measurement code. The framework reports implementation checks separately from measured attack effectiveness. Each privacy claim should state the attacker, dataset and scoring rule, report its controls and identify untested attacks.

Monitor-Validity Failures: LLM Safety Evidence Paths at Evolving Framework Boundaries
Authors: Junchi Liao
First: 2026-10-16T12:09:26+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T12:09:26+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Modern LLM applications rely on runtime monitors that inspect tool calls, traces, streams, detector outputs, and verifier inputs. This creates an interface-reliability problem: a framework path may remain operational while policy-relevant evidence never reaches its decision point. In one representative case, a regex detector returned a positive match, but the framework stranded the result before the block decision. This differs from jailbreaks and detector errors because the evidence is already recognized yet disconnected from enforcement. We study this repair-boundary signature with canary contracts: parent/fixed confirmation units that place a known payload only on the changed evidence surface and record monitor visibility together with the downstream decision for action-boundary cases. Applying the protocol to public framework repairs, we confirm 18 evidence-path failures across 11 LLM or agent safety project groups and four implementation ecosystems. Nine are established-boundary repairs, eight are new-surface policy-coverage gaps, and one is a boundary-only evidence case. Fourteen reach E1 enforcement boundaries: seven native action outcomes, six configured enforcement boundaries, and one synthetic-consumer consequence. The remaining cases affect verifier, safety-prompt, or observability channels. The cases form carrier, adapter, authority, and surface families and show how ordinary application behavior can remain intact while a safety-specific evidence path misses its decision. Exact revisions, deterministic outcomes, negative controls, and a frozen prospective audit document the confirmation rule. Monitor assumptions therefore need regression tests at the schemas, streams, tools, and adapters that frameworks evolve.

Refusal Is Not Robustness: Measuring Harmful Reachability in Autoregressive Language Models
Authors: Parv Kapoor, Teresa Fortes, Claudia Mamede, Rui Abreu, Jonathan Aldrich, Eunsuk Kang
First: 2026-10-15T23:45:21+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T23:45:21+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
LLM safety evaluations via jailbreaking typically index on attack success rate (ASR): whether a generated response violates safeguards. However, ASR only evaluates sampled outputs and provides little information on the underlying generation process. Autoregressive language models define distributions over possible outputs, and a sampled refusal can coexist with substantial probability mass on harmful generation paths. We study this hidden structure through bounded continuation analysis (BCA), which models autoregressive generation as a stochastic process over token prefixes and computes the finite-horizon probability that generation reaches a harmful continuation. BCA constructs a bounded continuation tree, labels terminal continuations with a semantic harm evaluator, and estimates harmful reachability using exact probabilistic model checking. BCA complements ASR by measuring how much harmful behavior remains accessible beneath a sampled safe response. This makes it useful for distinguishing models that look identical under measured ASR values, and allocating red-team effort toward regions of the generation process with greater hidden risk. Across four instruction-tuned LLMs and 100 JailbreakBench behaviors, we show that BCA effectively quantifies hidden harmful behavior and provides a useful signal to guide red teaming.

Abstract
Existing safety benchmarks rely largely on synthetic prompts with loosely grounded taxonomies and offer little focused analysis of severe crime categories. We introduce GUISE, a benchmark of criminal-assistance scenarios sourced from real-world cases and organised under a taxonomy from the UNODC International Classification of Crime for Statistical Purposes (ICCS). Each scenario is rendered under three linguistic framings and evaluated across five distinct cultures. Using our proposed two-tier evaluation framework, which judges both harmfulness and operational uplift, we find that: (i) framing alone drives unsafe compliance, with the mean unsafe compliance rate rising from 28.9% under direct requests to 66.4% under correction seeking (peaking at 97.3%; p < 0.05), as models correcting deliberately distorted details supply executable criminal steps; (ii) culturally adapted prompts elicit significantly more unsafe compliance than literal translations of the same requests in all four non-English languages (+7 to +18 points), a risk that translation-based benchmarks miss; and (iii) unsafe responses from the least-safe models carry strong Anglocentric registers, and the extent of this leakage increases monotonically with response harmfulness ($ρ$ = 0.46 to 0.63 across languages, all p < 0.001). GUISE offers a grounded resource and fine-grained diagnostics for strengthening refusal alignment across languages and cultures. We publish our code and data to facilitate future research.

Screwtape: Adversarial Transcript Editing for Monitor Red-Teaming
Authors: Gosha Kozhevnikov, Tyler Tracy
First: 2026-10-14T21:08:21+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-14T21:08:21+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
We present Screwtape, a pipeline that converts real agent transcripts into attacks that evade action-only monitors. An LLM attacker edits a transcript so that the agent causes a specified harm, such as leaking an API key or spawning an unmonitored agent. The edited actions are never executed, so we use an LLM verifier to reject attacks that are inconsistent with the original transcript or below a severity bar. The attacker revises the edit until the monitor no longer flags it. We applied Screwtape to 94 Claude Code and Codex transcripts from Redwood Research staff, optimizing against our own monitors. Across 252 runs, it produced 35 attacks that passed the verifier and evaded these monitors. Six of them, confirmed valid by human review, also evaded a held-out monitor provided by a frontier developer. We therefore demonstrate that a red-teamer without access to a developer's infrastructure can find confirmed failures of the developer's monitor. We expect to find more attacks when the pipeline runs inside the developer's infrastructure, with frontier attacker models that lack refusal safeguards and direct optimization against the production monitor. Developers can use these attacks to fix monitor blind spots, and evaluators can use them as counterexamples to safety claims about the monitor.

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents accomplish complex tasks through multi-turn interactions with external tools. However, malicious content embedded in user queries, tool descriptions, or environment observations can manipulate their decisions and induce unsafe actions. Adversarial co-evolution has emerged as an effective approach to strengthening LLM safety, but existing work primarily focuses on language-level safety or indirect prompt injection in web tasks. In this paper, we introduce AgentSafeEvo, a co-evolutionary reinforcement learning framework for agent safety built on an attacker-defender game spanning diverse tasks and attack types. Abstract

Abstract

Abstract
Automatic safety judges -- for example Llama Guard, or GPT-4o given a grading prompt -- produce the numbers behind reported jailbreak success rates, defense evaluations, and safety leaderboards. We ask whether these judges grade what a reply contains or how it sounds. We place a fixed piece of text before or after a model reply (a style wrapper) and leave the reply itself unchanged byte-for-byte, so any change in verdict is an error of the judge. Over 600 JailbreakBench replies (300 harmful, 300 refusals), 9 wrappers, and 8 judges, using paired tests and a measured per-judge repeat-disagreement rate (the noise floor), we find that most judges barely move but specific judges have specific, cheap blind spots. A token refusal ("I can't help with that") placed before the unchanged harmful text flips 19.9% of GPT-4o-mini's correct "unsafe" verdicts to "safe" (noise floor 0.5%; 18.2% under majority-of-three re-scoring), yet moves Claude only 0.4%. The deployed Llama Guard 4 guard model is evaded by an "educational course" preface on 12.3% of harmful replies, and the flips persist across repeats, while a second deployed guard (gpt-oss-safeguard-20b) is immune. Changing only the grading prompt to ignore tone cuts the GPT-4o-mini attack roughly tenfold on the same model, so the weakness lives in the judge, not in the content. Pure tone-only decoration that adds no new claim still flips up to 2.1% of verdicts on the vulnerable judges, several times their noise floors. On this artifact set the judges also disagree about which target model is safest, and a bootstrap shows the ranking is unstable before any wrapper is added. We release the data, wrappers, code, and per-verdict labels.

Abstract
Current LLM safety benchmarks are predominantly English-centric and often rely on translation, failing to capture country-specific harms. Moreover, they rarely evaluate a model's ability to detect culturally embedded sensitivities as distinct from universal harms. We introduce XL-SafetyBench, a suite of 5,500 test cases across 10 country-language pairs, comprising a Jailbreak Benchmark of country-grounded adversarial prompts and a Cultural Benchmark where local sensitivities are embedded within innocuous requests. Each item is constructed via a multi-stage pipeline that combines LLM-assisted discovery, automated validation gates, and dual independent native-speaker annotators per country. We report Attack Success Rate (ASR) alongside Neutral-Safe Rate (NSR) to separate principled refusal from non-engagement, and introduce the Cultural Sensitivity Rate (CSR) for recognizing embedded sensitivities. Evaluating 10 frontier and 27 local LLMs reveals two key findings. First, strong jailbreak robustness does not always come with strong cultural awareness, showing why the two should be evaluated separately. Second, local models exhibit a strong ASR-NSR trade-off (r = -0.81), suggesting that low attack success in several local models reflects non-engagement rather than safe refusal. XL-SafetyBench enables more nuanced, cross-cultural safety evaluation in the multilingual era.

Abstract
Model cards, system cards, and safety reports increasingly document LLM safety behaviors, yet safety evaluations typically start from predefined benchmarks rather than asking which documented claims those benchmarks actually test. We introduce LEST (LLM Evaluation through Specification-driven Testing), a framework that treats documented safety claims as specifications for black-box testing. In LEST, each claim determines which benchmark prompt tests it and whether a Refuse-to-Comply transition, where a model refuses a prompt but complies with its mutated version, counts as evidence against it. LEST mutates these prompts to search for counterexamples, manually validates each candidate, and traces validated counterexamples back to the claims they contradict. We evaluate LEST using the system cards of Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 with 97 seed prompts from SORRY-Bench across Mental Health and Self-Harm, Harmful and Violent Content, and Fairness and Bias. Of 56 identified safety claims, 29 (51.8%) are prompt-testable, but only 17 (58.6%) are exercised by an applicable benchmark prompt. Both models refuse >95% of benchmark prompts. Evaluating 1,836 mutated prompts on both models reveals 312 candidate Refuse-to-Comply transitions flagged by automated response classifiers. However, human validation confirms only 19 (6.1%) as both intent-preserving and genuinely compliant, showing automated evaluation substantially overestimates behavioral failures. Tracing these 19 transitions back to the claims reveals counterexamples for 6 of 9 covered claims for which refusal is an applicable oracle. Our results show that benchmark prompts may not be sufficient to validate all documented LLM safety behavior, and automated failure detection requires manual validation. LEST provides end-to-end traceability from natural-language safety documentation to executable tests and human-validated counterexamples.

SAEs Can Improve Unlearning: Dynamic Sparse Autoencoder Guardrails for Precision Unlearning in LLMs

Abstract
Machine unlearning is a promising approach to improve LLM safety by removing unwanted knowledge from the model. However, prevailing gradient-based unlearning methods suffer from issues such as high computational costs, hyperparameter instability, poor sequential unlearning capability, vulnerability to relearning attacks, low data efficiency, and lack of interpretability. While Sparse Autoencoders are well-suited to improve these aspects by enabling targeted activation-based unlearning, prior approaches underperform gradient-based methods. This work demonstrates that, contrary to these earlier findings, SAEs can significantly improve unlearning when employed dynamically. We introduce $\textbf{Dynamic DAE Guardrails}$ (DSG), a novel method for precision unlearning that leverages principled feature selection and a dynamic classifier. Our experiments show DSG substantially outperforms leading unlearning methods, achieving superior forget-utility trade-offs. DSG addresses key drawbacks of gradient-based approaches for unlearning -- offering enhanced computational efficiency and stability, robust performance in sequential unlearning, stronger resistance to relearning attacks, better data efficiency including zero-shot settings, and more interpretable unlearning.
Summary / 总结
Machine unlearning is a promising approach to improve LLM safety by removing unwanted knowledge from the model.
